A change has happened, and its result holds at the time spoken of.

私の車は壊れている。 My car is broken.

## How it is built

As for an action in progress: the て form, then いる. 壊れる → 壊れて → 壊れている.

## Which verbs

Verbs that name a change ending in a new state: 壊れる, break; 起きる, wake up; 来る, come; 行く, go; 持つ, take hold of; 着る, put on; 疲れる, get tired; 結婚する, marry; 死ぬ, die; 知る, come to know. With these, ている describes the state reached once the change is complete. Verbs of motion belong here: 行っている is that the person has gone and is there.

English often expresses these states with a verb of state or an adjective:

- 持っている, has (has taken hold of)
- 知っている, knows (has come to know)
- 分かっている, already understands
- 着ている, is wearing (has put on)
- 疲れている, is tired (has got tired)
- 起きている, is awake (has woken up)
- 行っている, is away there (has gone)

## Look-alikes

**An action in progress** (known). The form is the same, and usually the verb decides. If the action can go on with no end in view, as 読む, 待つ and 見る do, ている means it is going on. If the verb names a change that ends in a new state, ている means that state.

**A change under way.** To say the change itself is happening, Japanese needs another form, which depends on the verb: 死にかけている, is dying; 行く途中だ, is on the way.

**もう and まだ.** With もう the change has already happened: もう来ている, (he) is already here. With まだ and a negative it has not happened yet: まだ来ていない, (he) hasn't come yet. With a positive, まだ means "still": まだ起きてる？, are (you) still up?

**Transitive verbs and てある** (later). Many changes come in pairs: 開く, (something) opens, and 開ける, (someone) opens (something). On the first, ている gives the state: 窓が開いている, the window is open. On the second it is usually the action: 窓を開けている, (he) is opening the window; it can also mean that someone keeps the window open. Some transitive verbs, such as 持つ and 着る, give the state as a rule. てある on the second gives the state left by someone's deliberate act: 窓が開けてある, the window has been opened and left so.

## Examples

- 彼は車を持っている。 He has a car. (Tatoeba 104634)
- あなたに手紙が来ていますよ。 A letter has come for you. (Tatoeba 233677)
- トムはまだ来ていません。 Tom hasn't come yet. (Tatoeba 9002411)
- まだ起きてる？ Are you still up? (Tatoeba 2569601)

## Notes

1. "Know" is 知っている, but "don't know" is 知らない, not 知っていない.
2. 分かる can name the change, 分かった, (I) got it, or the state, 分かりますか, do (you) understand? 分かっている stresses that the state already holds.
3. A few verbs end a sentence only in this form and are learned as words, such as 似ている, resembles. Before a noun they often take た: 似た話, a similar story.
4. 行っている can also mean "goes regularly", as to a school. That is a later use, a habit.
5. With many subjects, a change can repeat and read as under way: 客が次々に来ている, guests keep arriving.
6. Kindaichi Haruhiko's classification of 1950 calls verbs like 読む durative (継続動詞) and verbs like 壊れる instantaneous (瞬間動詞).
