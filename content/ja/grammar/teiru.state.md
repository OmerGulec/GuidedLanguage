A change has happened, and its result holds at the time spoken of.

私の車は壊れている。 My car is broken.

## How it is built

As for an action in progress: the て form, then いる. 壊れる → 壊れて → 壊れている.

## Which verbs

Verbs of a change that is over in a moment: 壊れる, break; 起きる, wake up; 来る, come; 行く, go; 持つ, take hold of; 結婚する, marry; 死ぬ, die; 知る, come to know. With these, ている usually describes the state after the change. With a few, context decides: 着ている is usually "is wearing", and "is putting on" only when the act itself is in view.

Several of these are translated into English by a verb of state, not by "-ing":

- 持っている, has (has taken hold of)
- 知っている, knows (has come to know)
- 起きている, is awake (has woken up)
- 行っている, is away there (has gone)

## Look-alikes

**An action in progress** (known). The form is the same, and usually the verb decides. If the action can go on for a while, as 読む, 待つ and 見る do, ている means it is going on. If it is a change that is over in a moment, ている means its result.

**Not yet.** With まだ and a negative, the change has not happened: まだ来ていない, (he) hasn't come yet. The plain past 来なかった says only that he did not come on some occasion.

**Transitive verbs and てある** (later). Many changes come in pairs: 開く, (something) opens, and 開ける, (someone) opens (something). On the first, ている gives the state: 窓が開いている, the window is open. On the second, it is an action in progress: 窓を開けている, (he) is opening the window. てある on the second gives the state left by someone's deliberate act: 窓が開けてある, the window has been opened and left so.

## Examples

- 彼は車を持っている。 He has a car. (Tatoeba 104634)
- あなたに手紙が来ていますよ。 A letter has come for you. (Tatoeba 233677)
- トムはまだ来ていません。 Tom hasn't come yet. (Tatoeba 9002411)
- まだ起きてる？ Are you still up? (Tatoeba 2569601)

## Notes

1. "Know" is 知っている, but "don't know" is 知らない, not 知っていない.
2. 分かる already describes a state; 分かっている adds "already": (I) know that already.
3. A few verbs end a sentence only in this form and are learned as words, such as 似ている, resembles. Before a noun they take た: 似た話, a similar story.
4. 行っている can also mean "goes regularly", as to a school. That is a later use, a habit.
5. Kindaichi Haruhiko's classification of 1950 calls verbs like 読む durative (継続動詞) and verbs like 壊れる instantaneous (瞬間動詞).
