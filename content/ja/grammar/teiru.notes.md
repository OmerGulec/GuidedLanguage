# ている: sources compared

Read on 1 November 2026.

- Tae Kim, *A Guide to Japanese Grammar*, "Using ～ている for enduring states" in the te-form chapter. https://guidetojapanese.org/learn/grammar/teform
- Tofugu, "Japanese Verb Continuous Form ている". https://www.tofugu.com/japanese-grammar/verb-continuous-form-teiru/ It cites Makino and Tsutsui, *A Dictionary of Basic Japanese Grammar*, pp. 155–157, and Shirai (2000).
- Imabi, lesson 36, "The Progressive/Continuative State: ～ている". https://imabi.org/the-progressive-continued-state-te-iru-%ef%bd%9e%e3%81%a6%e3%81%84%e3%82%8b/

## Agreed by all three

- Built from the て form and いる; the whole conjugates as an ichidan verb.
- In casual speech the い drops: てる. Tae Kim and Imabi add that writing and formal speech keep it.
- With verbs of action that take time it is the progressive, "is doing".
- With some verbs it is the state left by a change, not a change in progress (結婚している, ついている, 立っている).

## Agreed by two

- After て, いる loses its own meaning: Tae Kim says it is used of anything, not only of people and animals; Imabi says it loses its literal sense.
- With a span of time it is "have been doing" (Tofugu, Imabi).
- Habits and repeated actions (Tofugu, Imabi). Tae Kim leaves this out.
- 知る is the change, so "know" is 知っている (Tae Kim, Tofugu). 分かっている means "already understand" (Tae Kim, Tofugu).
- 行っている, 来ている and 帰っている mean the move is over and the person is there (Tae Kim, Imabi).
- まだ with ていない is "not yet" (Tofugu, Imabi); もう with ている is "already" (Tofugu, and in examples by Tae Kim and Imabi).
- ある and いる do not take ている (Tofugu, Imabi).
- To say a change is under way you need another form: 死にかけている, "is dying" (Tofugu, Imabi).
- 疲れている, 太っている, やせている come out in English as adjectives (Tofugu, Imabi).
- Kansai speech uses いてる (Tofugu, Imabi). They describe it differently: Tofugu as being somewhere for a while, Imabi as a perfect progressive. The lesson only mentions it.

## Disagreements

- What decides between the two: the lesson first said "a change that is over in a moment", which gets 行く, 来る and 着る wrong. It now says "a change that ends in a new state", which fits Tae Kim's account of motion verbs and Imabi's separate section on them.
- 住んでいる: Tofugu places it with continuing actions, Imabi with resulting states. The meaning, "lives", is the same either way, so the lesson notes the split and does not take a side.
- Transitive verbs: Tofugu says テレビをつけている is a kept state, "have the TV on". Imabi says resulting states come mostly from intransitive verbs, and from transitive ones only when nothing is acted out (持っている, 含んでいる). The lesson gives the action as the usual reading for a transitive verb and the kept state as possible.
- 着ている: Imabi marks "is putting on" as wrong out of context and gives 着ているところ for that. A reviewing model thought "putting on" possible in context. The lesson gives only "is wearing".

## Only one source

- Repeated instant actions as progressive (叩いている, くしゃみをしている): Tofugu. Left out.
- Many subjects making a change read as repeated (多くの戦士が死んでいる): Tofugu. A footnote, with our own example.
- A record of past events (もう10回も日本に旅行している): Imabi. Belongs to the habit use, later.
- ないでいる and ずにいる: Imabi. Left out at this level.
- Plain form against ている for habits (毎朝食べる, 毎朝食べている): Tofugu. For the habit use.
- The preparation nuance of てある: Tae Kim. For the てある lesson.

## What each does well

- Tae Kim is short and goes straight to the verbs learners get wrong: 知る, 分かる and the motion verbs.
- Tofugu gives one picture for every use: ている says something switched on in the past and is still on. Its transitivity section and the two-way readings are the clearest of the three.
- Imabi is the most complete and classifies verbs by meaning. Its table of state verbs (割れている, 曲がっている, 欠けている) is the best list of words learners misread, and it is the only one with the record use and the negative forms.

## Not from these sources

The contrast with the dictionary form (読む is not "is reading") is our own addition. Imabi uses the term 瞬間動詞; crediting the classification to Kindaichi (1950) is ours. Both are standard.
